The World Food Encyclopedia

Our Scoring & Methodology

Exactly what our numbers mean, where they come from, and why no ranking should ever be the last word on dinner.

Where the scores come from

We don't invent scores — we translate them. Our rankings derive from TasteAtlas's two flagship global audience rankings:

  • 100 Best Dishes in the World — the 2025 edition, where Colombia's Lechona took the #1 spot.
  • 100 Worst Rated Foods in the World — the 2026 edition, led by Sweden's Pizza Vulkanen.

Both lists are built from global audience ratings, and we map each ranked dish's position onto a 10-point scale so the two lists can be read side by side:

  • Best list: #1 maps to 10.0, descending evenly to ~6.5 at #100.
  • Worst list: #1 maps to 3.5, descending evenly to ~1.0 at #100.
Important: unranked foods in our encyclopedia carry no official score. They appear with definitions and research, but a missing number is honest — it means the global rankings never weighed in.

How many foods are there, really?

There is no single fixed number — estimates range from about 50,000 to over 200,000 edible species, with effectively countless dishes once you count regional variations and preparations. It depends what you count:

  • Edible species: 50,000+ edible plant and animal species (FAO estimate); ~30,000 edible plant species documented globally; 200,000+ edible species per the Periodic Table of Food Initiative.
  • Commercial products: roughly 20,000–30,000 distinct food products sold worldwide.
  • Dishes and preparations: essentially infinite — cultures keep inventing new ones.

So our 1,203 documented dishes are a curated beginning, not a finish line. The encyclopedia grows dish by dish — and you can suggest the next one on the Submit a Pick page.

How TasteAtlas collects its ratings

TasteAtlas presents its lists as the product of roughly 750,000 ratings submitted by a global audience of food lovers — not a panel of critics, but a crowd. Here's how they describe the process, and how we read it:

  • Scale of collection: hundreds of thousands of individual dish ratings are gathered from visitors around the world, across dishes from every region.
  • Anti-bot measures: TasteAtlas applies automated detection to filter out bot votes, spam ratings, and other manipulation attempts — recognizing that internet polls live or die by the quality of their defenses.
  • National and regional balancing: the methodology reportedly weighs ratings to keep any single country's voting bloc from steamrolling the global result — a quiet but crucial safeguard in any popularity contest.
  • Audience, not experts: these are the verdicts of everyday eaters. That makes them culturally revealing, not academically definitive — and we treat them that way.
On community votes here: any voting widgets on this site store your votes only in your own browser via localStorage. They are never sent to us or anyone else — there is no server collecting them. Your votes are for your own amusement.

Why trust us — and why you shouldn't entirely

We'll take the "trust" part first: we are transparent about every number on this site. Every score traces back to a published TasteAtlas ranking, the mapping math is stated above in plain language, and unranked foods are never given invented scores. Our definitions and research are written from the same sources our footer names — TasteAtlas's global rankings, CNN reader polls, and UNESCO heritage listings.

Now the honest part — the limitations:

  • Taste is subjective. No global audience ranking can tell you what you will love. A dish ranked #87 worst might be your grandmother's pride, and she'd be right and the internet wrong — for you.
  • These rankings reflect a global audience, not a final verdict. Online ratings skew toward whoever shows up to vote: younger, more connected, more English-speaking than the planet as a whole. Regional cuisines with smaller internet footprints can be underrepresented.
  • Popularity is not quality. The most-voted dish is the most liked dish, not the most skillfully made one. Street food can outpoll haute cuisine, and that's fine — as long as nobody confuses the two.
  • Rankings freeze a moment. Tastes shift, new dishes emerge, and a 2025 or 2026 list is a snapshot, not scripture.

Our motto is in the footer: taste is personal. Use the rankings as a map, not a menu — then go disagree with us loudly. That's what they're for.
